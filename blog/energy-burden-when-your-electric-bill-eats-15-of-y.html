Energy Burden When Your Electric Bill Eats 15 of Y

James Patterson — 2026-05-20
Advertisement

I met a woman named Rosa at a community energy fair last year. She lived in a small apartment with her two kids. She worked full‑time as a home health aide. She told me her electric bill was $180 in the summer. Her rent was $1,200. Her take‑home pay was about $2,000 a month. That’s 9% of her income just for electricity. Add gas, and her energy burden was over 12%. She was skipping meals to pay her utility bills. She asked me if there was any help. I told her yes. But I also told her that 9% is insane. No one should pay that much.

The federal government considers energy burden “high” when a household spends more than 6% of its income on energy. “Severe” is over 10%. Rosa was in the severe category. She wasn’t wasteful. Her apartment was drafty. Her appliances were old. Her landlord didn’t care. She was stuck.

Energy burden is not evenly distributed. Low‑income households spend a much higher percentage of their income on energy than wealthy households. A family earning $30,000 a year might spend $2,000 on energy, or 6.7%. A family earning $150,000 a year might spend $3,000 on energy, or 2%. The poor pay more, both in percentage and often in absolute dollars because their homes are less efficient.

I’ve audited low‑income homes where the electric bill was $300 a month for a 1,000 square foot house. The problem was always the same. Old refrigerator. Electric resistance heat. No insulation. Single‑pane windows. The landlord paid for none of it. The tenant paid for all of it.

Rosa’s apartment had an ancient window AC unit that was pulling 12 amps. It was probably 30 years old. Its efficiency was maybe 6 SEER. A new window unit at 12 SEER would use half the electricity. But she couldn’t afford a new unit. She was trapped.

I told her about the Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program, LIHEAP. It’s a federal program that helps pay heating and cooling bills. She qualified. She applied. She got $400 toward her summer bills. That helped. But it didn’t fix the underlying problem.

She also qualified for weatherization assistance. The local nonprofit came and installed a new window AC, LED bulbs, and weatherstripping. They also gave her a power strip to kill vampire devices. Her electric bill dropped to $110. That’s a 39% reduction. Her energy burden dropped from 9% to 5.5%. She was no longer in the severe category.

That’s the power of weatherization. It’s not glamorous. It’s not solar panels. But it changes lives.

I have another client named James. He’s a veteran on a fixed income. His house was built in 1950. No insulation. Leaky ducts. A furnace from 1985. His winter gas bill was $300 a month. His summer electric bill was $250. His total energy burden was 18%. He was choosing between food and heat.

He qualified for LIHEAP and weatherization. The weatherization crew added attic insulation, sealed ducts, replaced his refrigerator, and installed a programmable thermostat. His winter gas bill dropped to $150. His summer electric bill dropped to $130. His energy burden dropped to 8%. Still high, but not severe. He told me he cried when he saw the first lower bill. He said he could finally afford to eat properly.

I see this again and again. Energy burden is a silent crisis. It affects health, food security, and mental well‑being. People in cold homes get sick more often. People in hot homes have worse asthma. Stress from bills leads to anxiety and depression.

The solutions exist. They’re not expensive at scale. But they require funding and political will.

Most utilities have low‑income assistance programs. They offer payment plans, discounts, or arrearage forgiveness. Some utilities have percentage of income payment plans, or PIPP. You pay 6% of your income for energy, and the utility covers the rest. That’s the gold standard.

Xcel Energy in Colorado has a program called Energy Assistance. It’s funded by a small surcharge on all customers. The surcharge is about $1 per month per household. That money is used to reduce bills for low‑income customers. It’s a good program.

The federal Weatherization Assistance Program provides free energy upgrades to low‑income households. The average home gets about $5,000 in upgrades. The energy savings pay back in about 5 years. And the benefits last for decades. It’s one of the most effective anti‑poverty programs we have.

But the program is underfunded. Only about 20% of eligible households receive weatherization each year. The waiting list is years long in some states.

Rosa was lucky. Her local agency had a grant from the state. She got help within three months. James waited 14 months. He almost gave up. He called me asking if he should take a payday loan to buy a space heater. I told him no. I helped him find emergency assistance from a charity. He got $200. It kept him going until his weatherization appointment.

If you’re struggling with high energy bills, here’s what you can do. First, call your utility. Ask for a payment plan or low‑income rate. Second, apply for LIHEAP. The application is usually online or through your local agency. Third, apply for weatherization. The wait may be long, but get on the list. Fourth, look for local charities. Salvation Army, Red Cross, and community action agencies often have emergency funds. Fifth, do the free stuff. Unplug vampire devices. Turn down the thermostat. Use fans instead of AC when possible.

I also tell people to check their electric bill for any assistance programs. Some utilities have a “medical baseline” allowance if someone in the home uses life‑support equipment. That can lower your rate.

Rosa now helps her neighbors apply for assistance. She became a community health worker. She says the training she got from the weatherization agency taught her everything. She can now spot a drafty window from across the room. She’s a force.

James sends me a Christmas card every year. He writes “thank you for not letting me give up.” That’s the best gift I get.

If you’re not low‑income, you can still help. Donate to your local community action agency. Volunteer with Habitat for Humanity. Support energy efficiency funding in your state. And be aware that your own energy burden is likely low. Be grateful.
