Solar Panel Payback Real Numbers 2026

2026-07-23 · 2 min read · General
General

My solar panels paid for themselves in five years. Not the eight years the installer predicted. Not the ten years the skeptics warned about. Five. And I have the utility bills to prove it. Year one savings: $1,180. Year two: $1,240. Year three: $1,310. Year four: $1,280. Year five: $1,350. Total five-year savings: $6,360. Installation cost after federal tax credit: $12,600. At five years, I was at $6,360 in savings. At six years, I crossed into profit. And every year after that is free money.

I live in Phoenix. This matters. Solar production depends on sun exposure, and Phoenix has 299 sunny days a year. My 8-kilowatt system produces 13,000 kilowatt-hours annually. That covers 85% of my usage. The other 15% comes from the grid at night, during monsoon storms, and in December when the days are short. But net metering credits my excess daytime production against nighttime usage. The math works.

The federal tax credit was 30% when I installed. That dropped my $18,000 system to $12,600. In 2026, the credit is 26%. Still significant, but declining. Some states add rebates. Arizona does not. But my utility offers a $1,000 rebate for new solar installations. I missed it by three months. That still stings.

Net metering is the key. Without it, solar makes no sense for most people. Net metering means the utility buys my excess power at retail rates. During the day, my meter spins backward. At night, it spins forward. The difference is my bill. In summer, I produce more than I use. I bank credits. In winter, I use more than I produce. I draw from the bank. Over a year, it balances. But some states are reducing net metering rates. California's NEM 3.0 slashed compensation by 75%. If Arizona follows, my payback would stretch to 12 years. That is the risk.

Maintenance is minimal. I rinse the panels twice a year. That is it. No moving parts. No fuel. No noise. The inverter might need replacement at year 12. That costs $2,000. But by then, I will have saved $15,000. The inverter is a rounding error.

The environmental benefit is real but secondary. My system prevents 4.2 tons of CO2 annually. Over 25 years, that is 105 tons. Not life-changing on a global scale, but not nothing. And in Phoenix, where the grid is powered partly by coal, every kilowatt of solar helps. It helps the air. It helps the grid. It helps my conscience.

If you are considering solar, run the numbers for your location. Phoenix? No-brainer. Seattle? Different math. Check your sun hours. Check your utility rates. Check net metering rules. And get multiple quotes. I got five. The range was $14,000 to $22,000 for the same system. That $8,000 difference is pure salesmanship. Shop around. The sun is free. The panels should not cost more than necessary.

— David Reynolds, Phoenix, AZ

David is a homeowner who tracks his solar production like a day trader tracks stocks. He believes in renewable energy, ROI, and never paying for sunlight.
