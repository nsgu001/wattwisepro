The Time I Forgot About My Dryer Vent and Almost B

James Patterson — 2026-04-20
Advertisement

The smell was the first clue. A musty, burning smell that started faint and got worse over a few weeks. I ignored it. I thought maybe the neighbors were burning something. Then my dryer stopped drying. Clothes came out hot but wet. I had to run them through twice. My electric bill crept up. I was using twice the electricity for the same laundry. Finally, I pulled the dryer away from the wall and looked at the vent hose. It was crushed. I disconnected it and saw the problem. A solid wall of lint. The hose was clogged solid. I had never cleaned it in seven years.

I felt like an idiot. An energy auditor who neglects his own dryer vent. The irony was not lost on me. I cleared the lint with a vent brush kit. It took about fifteen minutes. The difference was immediate. The dryer ran for forty minutes instead of eighty. My bill dropped about $15 a month. More importantly, I probably avoided a fire. Lint is incredibly flammable. Dryer fires cause hundreds of millions of dollars in damage every year. I was lucky.

This is one of the simplest energy fixes you can do, but almost nobody does it. The Department of Energy says a clogged dryer vent can reduce efficiency by 30‑50%. That means if you run your dryer 200 times a year, you’re wasting dozens of dollars and hours of time. The fix costs $10 for a brush kit and twenty minutes of your time.

I started paying attention to dryers after my own near‑disaster. I learned that the vent hose should be as short and straight as possible. Every bend traps lint. The smooth metal hoses are better than the flexible plastic or foil ones. The plastic hoses can also sag and collect water, which leads to mold. I replaced my old plastic hose with a rigid metal one. Cost $25. My drying time dropped another five minutes per load.

I also learned about moisture sensors. Many dryers have a sensor that detects when clothes are dry and shuts off automatically. If that sensor is coated with lint or fabric softener residue, it won’t work right. I cleaned mine with a q‑tip and alcohol. That helped too.

Another client named Maria had a dryer that took over an hour to dry a small load. I checked her vent. It was kinked and full of lint. I cleared it and cut her drying time in half. She estimated she had been running her dryer 300 hours a year. That’s about 900 kilowatt‑hours at $0.14 per kWh, or $126. After the fix, her drying time dropped to 150 hours, saving $63 a year. She thanked me and said she felt silly for not checking sooner.

I see this pattern all the time. People assume their appliances are working fine because they run. They don’t realize how much energy they’re wasting. A dryer with a clogged vent runs longer, gets hotter, and wears out faster. It also heats up the laundry room, which makes your AC work harder in summer. The hidden costs add up.

If you have a gas dryer, a clogged vent is even more dangerous. Carbon monoxide can back up into your house. You won’t smell it. You’ll just get headaches and feel tired. That’s a serious health risk. Every gas dryer should have a carbon monoxide detector nearby. I put one in my laundry room after learning this.

The best way to prevent buildup is to clean your lint filter after every load. That’s obvious. But also wash the lint filter with soap and water every few months. Fabric softener leaves a film that traps more lint. And once a year, disconnect the vent hose and clean the whole line with a brush. You can buy a kit for $10‑$20. Some people use a leaf blower, but that can damage the hose.

I did a test in my own house before and after cleaning. My dryer used 3.2 kWh per load before cleaning. After, it used 1.9 kWh. That’s a 40% reduction. At $0.14 per kWh, that’s $0.18 per load saved. With 8 loads a week, that’s $1.44 a week, $75 a year. Plus my clothes came out less wrinkled because they weren’t tumbling for an extra forty minutes.

I have a client named Dave who runs a home inspection business. He says clogged dryer vents are in his top five most common safety issues. He sees them in new houses too. Builders sometimes run long, kinked vents with cheap materials. Homeowners never think about them. Dave recommends a rigid metal vent with a maximum length of 25 feet. Subtract 5 feet for every bend. If your vent is longer than that, you need a booster fan.

Another thing I learned is that some dryers have a “wrinkle guard” feature that tumbles the clothes periodically after the cycle ends. That uses electricity too. I turned mine off. If you can’t get to the laundry right away, just run the dryer again for a few minutes later. That’s more efficient than running the wrinkle guard for hours.

I also started hanging more clothes to dry. A folding drying rack costs $20. It pays for itself in a few months. I hang my jeans, heavy sweaters, and sheets on the rack. They come out smelling fresh and last longer because they’re not tumbling in heat. My dryer usage dropped by about a third.

The bottom line is that your dryer is one of the biggest energy users in your house, after your HVAC and water heater. A typical family spends $100‑$200 a year drying clothes. You can cut that by 20‑50% with simple maintenance and a few behavior changes. Clean the vent. Clean the lint filter. Use the moisture sensor. Hang dry when you can. Don’t be like me and ignore it for seven years.
