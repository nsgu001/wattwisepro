10 Ways to Lower Your Electric Bill Without Spendi

James Patterson — 2026-04-24
Advertisement

I met a guy at a barbecue last summer. He told me his electric bill was out of control. He said he was thinking about buying solar panels or a new AC. He asked me what I recommended. I asked him if he had tried the free stuff first. He looked at me like I was speaking a foreign language. Free stuff? What free stuff?

Most people skip the low‑hanging fruit. They want a big solution. Solar. Heat pump. New windows. Those things can help. But they cost thousands of dollars. Meanwhile, there are at least a dozen ways to lower your electric bill that cost nothing. Zero dollars. Just behavior changes. I’ve used them all. They work.

Here are ten of my favorites.

Number one: unplug vampire devices. Anything with a wall wart transformer, a clock display, or a standby light draws power even when turned off. Your phone charger. Your cable box. Your coffee maker. Your gaming console. Your computer monitor. The average home has about 20 of these. Together, they can account for 5‑10% of your electric bill. That’s $100‑$200 per year for many families. Unplug them when not in use. Or plug them into a power strip and turn off the strip. Free savings.

I had a client named Derek who was convinced his bill was too high. He had done everything. LEDs. Smart thermostat. He couldn’t figure it out. I asked him to walk around his house at night and count the little red and green lights. He found 23 devices. We unplugged them. His bill dropped $25 the next month. He was shocked.

Number two: shift your laundry to off‑peak times. Even without a time‑of‑use rate, running your dryer at 8 PM might cost the same as running it at 10 AM. But if you have TOU, the savings can be significant. In my utility, off‑peak is 7 PM to 1 PM the next day. Running the dryer at 9 PM instead of 6 PM saves me about $0.50 per load. That’s $15 a month. Free.

Number three: air dry your clothes. This is the extreme version. Hang your clothes on a rack or a line. A clothes dryer is one of the biggest energy users in a home. A single load uses about 3‑5 kWh. At $0.15 per kWh, that’s $0.45‑$0.75 per load. If you do 8 loads a week, that’s $14‑$24 per month. An indoor drying rack costs $20. It pays for itself in a month. Then it’s free.

Number four: raise your thermostat in summer, lower it in winter. Every degree of cooling above 78°F saves about 3% on your cooling bill. Every degree of heating below 68°F saves about 3% on your heating bill. Set your thermostat to 78 in summer and 68 in winter. Wear shorts or a sweater. That’s free.

Number five: use your ceiling fans correctly. Fans cool people, not rooms. Run them counter‑clockwise in summer to create a downdraft. That allows you to raise your thermostat by about 4°F without feeling warmer. Turn them off when you leave the room. Fans use electricity. Don’t waste it.

Number six: close your blinds and curtains. On sunny summer days, sunlight heats your home. Close blinds on south and west facing windows. That reduces your cooling load. On sunny winter days, open them to let the sun in. That reduces your heating load. Free.

Number seven: run your dishwasher only when full. A dishwasher uses about 1‑2 kWh per load, plus the energy to heat the water. Running it half‑empty is wasteful. Also, skip the heat dry cycle. Open the door and let the dishes air dry. Free.

Number eight: wash clothes in cold water. Heating water accounts for 90% of the energy in a laundry load. Use cold water detergent. Wash everything in cold. You’ll save about $0.30 per load. If you do 8 loads a week, that’s $10 a month. Free.

Number nine: defrost your refrigerator. Frost buildup makes your fridge work harder. If you see ice, defrost it. Also, check the seals. A dollar bill should not slide out easily when the door is closed. If it does, the seal is leaking. That’s not free to fix, but cleaning the seal is.

Number ten: change your HVAC filter. A dirty filter makes your blower work harder. It reduces airflow. It can increase your energy use by 5‑15%. A basic filter costs $5. You can get them even cheaper in bulk. But the act of changing it costs nothing. Do it every 1‑3 months.

I used to think these tips were too small to matter. Then I added them up. For a typical family, the annual savings from these ten things can be $300‑$600. That’s real money. And it costs nothing to start.

Derek, the guy with the vampire devices, now does all ten. He says his bill is $80 lower than last year. That’s almost $1,000. He didn’t buy solar. He didn’t replace his AC. He just changed his habits.

But let me be honest. Some of these tips require effort. Changing your laundry schedule takes planning. Raising your thermostat takes willpower. Air drying clothes takes space. Not everyone can do everything. That’s fine. Pick the ones that fit your life.

I had a client named Sarah who lived in a small apartment. She couldn’t air dry clothes. She couldn’t change her filter. But she could unplug vampire devices and run her dishwasher only when full. She saved $15 a month. That’s not nothing.

Another client, a retired couple, adopted all ten. They said it became a game. They compete to see who can find more vampire devices. They’re down to 0.4 kWh per day in standby power. That’s amazing.

Now, I know what you’re thinking. This is boring. You want a big solution. But here’s the thing. Even if you install solar, you should still do these things. They reduce your usage. A smaller usage means a smaller solar system. That saves you money upfront.
