Vampire Devices The Always-On Electronics Costing

James Patterson — 2026-04-26
Advertisement

I was at a friend’s house a few months ago. He was complaining about his electric bill. “It’s always high,” he said. “Even when we’re on vacation.” He showed me his bill for a week when the house was empty. It was $18. For a week with nobody home. That’s $72 a month just for doing nothing. I asked him what was plugged in. He shrugged. We walked around with a kill‑a‑watt meter. Microwave. Coffee maker. Cable box. Two routers. A printer. Three phone chargers. A desktop computer in sleep mode. A TV in standby. A soundbar. A gaming console. A smart speaker. A charging dock for a vacuum. The total? 85 watts. Continuously. That’s 74 kilowatt‑hours per month. At his rate of $0.14 per kWh, that’s $10.36 a month. Just for devices doing nothing.

These are vampire devices. Also called phantom loads, standby power, or wall warts. They draw power even when turned off. Some draw power even when they look off. Your cable box is never really off. It’s waiting for a signal from the remote. Your gaming console is updating games in sleep mode. Your microwave has a clock. Your smart speaker is always listening.

The average home has 20‑30 vampire devices. The typical vampire load is 50‑100 watts continuous. Over a year, that’s 438 to 876 kilowatt‑hours. At the US average rate of $0.16 per kWh, that’s $70 to $140 per year. In high‑rate states like California or Hawaii, it can be $200‑$300 per year. For nothing.

I have a client named Lisa who was convinced her house had a wiring problem. Her bill was too high, she said. I did a full audit. The house was fine. But I found 120 watts of vampire load. An old desktop computer in the basement. Two DVRs. Five phone chargers. Three routers. A printer in standby. A spare refrigerator in the garage. We unplugged the spare fridge. We put the computers and chargers on power strips. We turned off the DVRs at night. Her bill dropped $28 the next month. She was relieved it wasn’t a wiring issue. She was annoyed that she had been paying for nothing for years.

The worst offenders are devices with external power supplies. Those black bricks that plug into the wall. They convert AC to DC. They get warm. That warmth is wasted energy. A phone charger with nothing attached still draws about 0.5 watts. That’s small. But multiply by 10 chargers, and that’s 5 watts continuous. 5 watts times 24 hours times 365 days is 43.8 kWh. At $0.15, that’s $6.57 a year. Not huge, but it’s something.

The real vampires are entertainment systems. A cable box can draw 15‑30 watts even when “off.” A game console in standby can draw 10‑20 watts. A soundbar can draw 5‑10 watts. A TV can draw 1‑5 watts in standby. Add it up: 50 watts easily. That’s 438 kWh per year, $66 at $0.15.

Computers are another big category. A desktop computer in sleep mode still draws 5‑15 watts. A laptop charger plugged in but not attached draws 0.5‑2 watts. A router and modem combo draws 10‑20 watts continuously. You need those for internet, so you can’t unplug them. But you can unplug the computer when not in use.

The spare fridge is a classic vampire. A second refrigerator in the garage or basement can use 300‑800 kWh per year, depending on age. That’s $45‑$120 per year. If it’s old, it could be even more. I’ve seen fridges from the 1980s using 1,500 kWh per year. That’s $225 a year. For a fridge that holds a six‑pack and some leftovers.

I tell my clients to do a vampire hunt. Once a year, walk through your house with a kill‑a‑watt meter or a smart plug. Measure everything that’s plugged in. Write down the wattage. Multiply by 24 hours by 365 days. Divide by 1,000 to get kWh. Multiply by your electric rate. That’s the annual cost. You’ll be surprised.

The spare fridge is the biggest. A 1990s fridge in the garage is a money pit. Unplug it. Or replace it with a small, efficient unit if you really need it.

Now, how do you stop the vampires? The cheapest way is to unplug. But that’s annoying. The better way is to use power strips. Plug your computer, monitor, printer, and phone chargers into one strip. Turn it off when you leave. For entertainment systems, use a smart power strip. It has a “master” outlet for your TV. When the TV turns off, the other outlets turn off too. So your cable box, soundbar, and game console turn off automatically.

Some devices need to stay on. Routers, modems, security cameras, alarm systems, smart speakers if you use them at night. That’s fine. You can’t eliminate all vampires. But you can reduce them.

I did my own vampire audit last year. I found 73 watts of continuous load. I put my entertainment system on a smart strip. I unplugged two phone chargers in the guest room. I turned off my desktop computer completely at night instead of sleeping it. My baseline dropped to 38 watts. Saving 35 watts continuous. That’s 307 kWh per year. At my rate of $0.147, that’s $45 a year. For twenty minutes of work.

I have a tool that helps you calculate your vampire load.

The tool can’t measure vampires directly, but it can show you your lowest usage days. Compare a day when you’re home to a day when you’re away. The difference is your vampire load plus your refrigerator.

For a more precise measurement, I recommend a kill‑a‑watt meter. It’s about $20. Plug it in, plug your device into it, and it shows you the wattage. That’s the best $20 you’ll spend.
